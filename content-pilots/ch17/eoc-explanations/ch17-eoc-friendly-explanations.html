Smith Chapter 17 — What do I need to do?

Editing draft • EOC questions 17.35–17.79

Use this beside your question sheet. Find your question number, then read the matching part. Each explanation tells you what to notice and what to change in the drawing. For questions asking for a mechanism, use the product as your destination, then show the electron arrows that get you there.

A useful starting pattern: For an aldehyde or ketone treated with NaBH₄, change C=O into C–OH and add H to that same carbon. Keep the carbon skeleton. An aldehyde becomes a primary alcohol; a ketone becomes a secondary alcohol. This pattern does not cover every compound containing C=O.

Reading the shorthand: Me means CH₃; Et means CH₂CH₃; Ph means a benzene ring attached at one carbon. “Workup” means the finishing step, usually water or dilute acid, that turns O⁻ into OH. A wedge points toward you; a dashed bond points away.

17.35 — Find the carbonyl group in each model

A contains a ketone. B contains an ester. That difference decides whether you add one carbon group, add two, or get no reaction.

  • A(a): NaBH₄ turns the ketone into a secondary alcohol. Change C=O to C–OH and put H on that carbon.
  • A(b): LiAlH₄ gives the same alcohol after water is added. The carbon skeleton stays the same.
  • A(c): CH₃MgBr adds one CH₃ to the C=O carbon. Change C=O to C–OH after the water step.
  • A(d): PhLi adds one phenyl group to the C=O carbon. That carbon also becomes the OH carbon.
  • A(e): No reaction here. This oxidizing mixture does not turn this ketone into another product under the chapter’s conditions.
  • B(a): No reaction here. NaBH₄ leaves this ester alone.
  • B(b): LiAlH₄ turns the ester’s C=O end into CH₂OH. The OCH₃ piece comes off and becomes methanol; keep the separate ether elsewhere in the molecule.
  • B(c): Excess CH₃MgBr adds two CH₃ groups at the ester’s C=O carbon. Remove the OCH₃ group and turn the carbonyl oxygen into OH.
  • B(d): Excess PhLi adds two phenyl groups at that same carbon. Remove OCH₃ and finish with OH there.
  • B(e): No reaction here. Neither the ester nor the ether is the alcohol or aldehyde this oxidizing mixture changes.

17.36 — Build a larger alcohol from small alcohols

Make the carbon piece you want to add into a Grignard reagent. Make the other piece into an aldehyde or ketone. Join them, then add water.

  • (a): Make acetaldehyde from ethanol using PCC. Add an ethyl group using EtMgBr, then water, to make 2-butanol. Make EtMgBr from another portion of ethanol using PBr₃, then Mg.
  • (b): Add MeMgBr to acetaldehyde, then water, to make isopropyl alcohol. Use PCC to turn it into acetone, then add MeMgBr and water again to make tert-butyl alcohol. The methyl reagent comes from methanol using PBr₃, then Mg.
  • (c): Make isopropyl alcohol as in (b), then use PBr₃ and Mg to make its Grignard reagent. Add formaldehyde, then water: this puts a new CH₂OH on the isopropyl group. Make formaldehyde from methanol using PCC.
  • (d): Add EtMgBr to ethylene oxide, then water. Opening that little three-membered ring adds two carbons, ending in OH, to give 1-butanol. Make ethylene oxide from ethanol by dehydration to ethene, then mCPBA.

17.51 — The ketone and ester need different numbers of additions

The ketone takes one methyl, then becomes an alcohol. The ester takes two methyls at its carbonyl carbon. After the first addition, the oxygen attached to the original skeleton leaves. After the second, that carbon becomes the OH carbon of tert-butyl alcohol. Water turns the separated O⁻ groups into OH.

For the arrows: move electrons from the methyl reagent to C=O carbon and move the C=O π electrons to oxygen. At the ester, bring oxygen’s electrons back to make C=O and break the bond to the leaving oxygen before adding the second methyl.

17.73 — The new O⁻ can close an epoxide

Vinyl lithium first adds its vinyl group to C=O, leaving O⁻. That oxygen then attacks the neighboring carbon carrying Cl, from the opposite side, and pushes Cl off. Draw the resulting epoxide; keep the incoming vinyl C=C.

17.74 — Build salmeterol in manageable pieces

  • Use (R)-CBS on A’s ketone to make the alcohol orientation shown, then protect both OH groups with TBDMS. Keep that stereocenter’s orientation through the remaining steps.
  • Use NaH on B’s alcohol, then the dibromohexane piece, to join them through oxygen while leaving Br at the far end. Replace that terminal Br with NH₂ using ammonia.

17.75 — The carbon farther from oxygen can have the larger shift

A resonance form is another way to draw the same atoms with the electrons in different positions. Draw the form with O⁻ and a positive charge at the far end of C=O–C=C. The far alkene carbon is relatively short of electrons, so it appears at the larger ¹³C shift, 150.5 ppm. The nearer alkene carbon appears at 122.5 ppm. Being physically nearer to oxygen is not enough to predict these two shifts.

To draw that resonance form, move C=O’s π electrons to O and move the adjacent C=C electrons toward the carbonyl carbon. Do not move any atoms.

17.76 — Use the carbon beside CN to make the new bond

17.77 — A resonance form reveals a second place to react

  • P, the minor product: The benzyl carbon attaches to formaldehyde. Water gives Ph–CH₂–CH₂OH: the straightforward one-carbon extension.
  • N, the major product: A resonance form puts the reactive electron pair on an ortho ring carbon—the ring position immediately next to the original side chain. That carbon attaches to formaldehyde; proton transfers restore the aromatic ring. The old benzyl end becomes CH₃, and the new neighboring group becomes CH₂OH, giving ortho-methylbenzyl alcohol.

17.78 — The same nitrogen makes two new bonds

The nitrogen of NH₂OH first attacks the far end of one C=O–C=C unit. After proton transfers, that same nitrogen attacks the far end of the other unit within the molecule, closing the bridge. The final product keeps C=O and has an N–OH bridge.

For each addition, start with nitrogen’s lone pair, shift the alkene electrons toward C=O, and shift the C=O electrons to oxygen. Use proton transfers to restore C=O. The new carbon bonds are made by N, not O.

17.79 — This cyclic amide ends as an amine, not an alcohol

The carbon that began as C=O ends with two new ethyl groups and no oxygen. Keep the ring’s C–N bond: the ring stays closed.

First, ethyl adds to C=O. In Smith’s mechanism, oxygen leaves after proton transfer while N helps form C=N⁺. A second ethyl then attacks that carbon and the C=N electrons move back to N. Use this particular amide pathway; do not leave OH on the product as you would for an ester plus excess Grignard reagent.
