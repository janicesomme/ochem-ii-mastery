NO FEAR OCHEM · CHAPTER 17 · V2 — PEAKS CHECKED

Same reaction.
Three ways to ask.

Find the pattern. Make the move. Use it whichever part of the reaction is missing.

1 · Learn these two pairs

Keep the condition with the fact: these patterns apply when NaBH₄ reduces an aldehyde or ketone. Other reagents can make those same starting groups react differently.

An aldehyde becomes a primary alcohol.

Look for the carbon with both a double bond to O and a bond to H. That is the aldehyde site. NaBH₄ turns it into a primary alcohol: the carbon carrying OH is attached to just one other carbon.

A ketone becomes a secondary alcohol.

Look for the carbon double-bonded to O with a carbon group on each side. That is a ketone. NaBH₄ turns it into a secondary alcohol: the carbon carrying OH stays attached to two other carbons.

The drawing move: change the double bond to oxygen into a single bond. Add H to oxygen and H to the same carbon. Keep every existing carbon–carbon connection.

Where is the new H on carbon? Skeletal drawings usually leave it unwritten. It is still there. An unlabeled line end or corner represents carbon, with enough H to give carbon four bonds.

Amber outlines mark the site that changes. They do not represent electron charge. Methanol is the solvent used in these examples.

Show the skeletal answer

No reaction under these conditions: NaBH₄ in methanol leaves this ester unchanged. The repeated starting structure below shows that no alcohol product forms. Seeing a double bond to oxygen is not enough—you must identify the group.

The check to carry into your next question

  1. Find the carbon double-bonded to oxygen.
  2. Check what else is attached to that carbon. Is it an aldehyde, a ketone, or another group?
  3. Read the reagent. With NaBH₄ in these examples, aldehydes give primary alcohols and ketones give secondary alcohols.
  4. Decide which part the question has hidden: product, reagent, or starting material.
