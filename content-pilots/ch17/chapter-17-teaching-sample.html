01 · THE REACTION IN ONE FRIENDLY SENTENCE

NaBH₄ turns an aldehyde into a primary alcohol.

Goal

Find the reacting group and draw its change, even inside a large molecule.

What should you notice first?

First, find a carbon double-bonded to oxygen and also bonded to H. That end group is an aldehyde.

Look at what changed: the C=O double bond becomes a C–O single bond. That carbon gains H, and the oxygen gains H. The end group is now CH₂OH.

A primary alcohol has its OH-bearing carbon attached to one other carbon. Here, that carbon is the CH₂ in CH₂OH.

Read the arrow: NaBH₄ is sodium borohydride, the reagent that brings about the reduction. CH₃OH is methanol, the solvent shown in this example. Use the conditions printed in your question.

02 · SITES THAT MATTER

Same change. Bigger molecule.

This molecule looks busy. Start at the aldehyde: the carbon with both C=O and a bond to H. NaBH₄ turns that group into a primary alcohol, with CH₂OH at the end.

● Blue carbon: electron-poor● Red oxygen: electron-richGrey: unchanged in this reaction

Why these colours? Oxygen pulls shared electrons towards itself. This leaves the carbon electron-poor and the oxygen electron-rich. The outline marks the region to inspect; it does not mean charge.

The rings and the connecting chain stay the same under these conditions. Keep their shape as you draw the product. Work on the highlighted end.

Original illustrative molecule: 4-(4-cyclohexylphenyl)butanal. This is not a reproduced or verified Smith EOC structure. The grey region is unchanged for this particular reaction; it is not a universal “ignore” label.

Your turn: find it without the colours

You have NaBH₄ in CH₃OH.

  1. Point to the aldehyde.
  2. Mark the bond that changes.
  3. Draw the product, keeping the rest of the skeleton.
Check your thinking

The aldehyde is at the far right. Change its C=O to C–OH and add H to that carbon. The end becomes CH₂OH. Both rings and the chain stay intact.

03 · NO FEAR PASSAGE + REUSABLE TOOL

Give the carbonyl carbon H—or give it carbon.

A carbonyl group is C=O: carbon double-bonded to oxygen. In this first pair of reaction families, your decision is what the reagent adds to that carbon.

NaBH₄: add H

NaBH₄ turns an aldehyde into a primary alcohol, or a ketone into a secondary alcohol.

A ketone has its C=O carbon bonded to two carbons. Its product is a secondary alcohol: the OH-bearing carbon remains attached to two carbons.

Carbon count: no new carbon is added.

A Grignard reagent: add a carbon group

A Grignard reagent contains a carbon–magnesium bond, often written R–MgBr. Its carbon group forms a new bond to the C=O carbon. The later water or acid step turns O⁻ into OH.

Carbon count: the incoming group adds carbon to the product.

Tool: decide what goes onto C=O

  1. Find every C=O. Check what is attached to each carbonyl carbon. An aldehyde has H there; a ketone has carbon groups on both sides.
  2. Read the reagent. Is this the NaBH₄ pattern, or a carbon-adding Grignard reagent?
  3. Check for an interfering group. With a Grignard reagent, an unprotected O–H or N–H can consume reagent by transferring H⁺.
  4. Draw the change and check the carbon count. For the aldehyde/ketone patterns above, C=O becomes C–OH after the appropriate proton-transfer step.
Keep the boundary visible: a carbonyl attached to O, N, or Cl is a different starting pattern. Do not extend this tool to every C=O. Carboxylic acid derivatives need their own reagent and leaving-group checks.

Common trap

Do not add a new carbon when using NaBH₄. It adds hydrogen in this pattern. Check the carbon count before and after.

Quick check

You reduce an aldehyde with NaBH₄. Does the product have more carbon atoms?

Show the answer

No. NaBH₄ adds hydrogen in this reaction. It does not add a carbon group.

04 · INFO CARD

Smith 17.48: check the O–H before building the Grignard reagent.

What is this testing? Whether another group in the molecule stops your planned reaction.

Look here first: the starting material has an O–H bond as well as a carbon–bromine bond used to make the Grignard reagent.

Why it matters: a Grignard reagent is also a strong base—it can take H⁺ from O–H. That consumes the carbon–magnesium reagent before the planned carbon–carbon bond can form.

Think before drawing: which bond could supply H⁺? What would that do to the carbon–magnesium bond you need?

Your next decision: how could you stop that interfering reaction temporarily and still restore the OH at the end?

Ochem II point: inspect the whole molecule once before deciding which region you can leave alone.
