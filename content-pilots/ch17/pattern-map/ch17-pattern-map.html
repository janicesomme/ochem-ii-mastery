Chapter 17 — Patterns Behind the Questions

Starter map for Janice • Smith EOC 17.35–17.79 • Teaching wording still open for editing

The goal is to help a student ask: “Which familiar move is this question asking me to use?”

This draft proposes 12 teaching groups, plus 5 supporting skill groups. These are an editorial starting point, not a proven minimum toolkit. Several groups work together in longer questions. The index maps every question and the separately identified parts in the companion draft; it is not a count of independent skills or of final products to draw.

What you see first: the reacting group, the reagent, and any nearby group that can change the outcome. A larger molecule can use the same move, but its extra groups must be checked before treating them as background.

Common trap: treating every C=O the same. What is attached to its carbon—and which reagent is present—decides the move.

Quick check: would NaBH₄ change both the ketone and the ester in 17.41(a)? Answer: the ketone changes; the ester stays. The matching C=O shape is not the whole decision.

Pattern cards

P1 — Choose the site that reacts

Notice first: Find each C=O and check what is attached to its carbon. Also look for OH and C=C.

The move: Match the reagent to each group before changing any bonds. “No reaction” is a valid result.

Different appearance: A model, a line drawing, or a large molecule can contain the same reactive group.

A difference that matters: An ester is not a ketone. An extra OH can destroy a Grignard reagent; a nearby C=C can create another reaction site.

Starter practice sequence: 17.35A(a) → 17.41(a) → 17.42(a)

Same NaBH₄ decision, with more surrounding structure. Compare 17.35B(a), where the ester stays unchanged.

P2 — Turn an aldehyde or ketone into an alcohol

Notice first: Look for an aldehyde or ketone and a reducing reagent such as NaBH₄.

The move: Change C=O to C–OH. Add H to that same carbon.

Different appearance: The C=O may sit at a chain end, inside a ring, or on a large skeleton.

A difference that matters: This drawing move does not cover esters or amides. H₂/Pd may also change C=C under the stated conditions.

Starter practice sequence: 17.37(a) → 17.37(b) → 17.35A(a)

Repeat the aldehyde reduction with another reagent; then notice that a ketone gives a secondary alcohol.

P3 — Reduce an ester, acid, or amide

Notice first: Look for C=O bonded to O or N, then check the hydride reagent.

The move: With LiAlH₄, an ester or acid carbonyl end becomes CH₂OH. An amide C=O becomes CH₂ while its C–N bond stays. A nitrile, C≡N, becomes CH₂NH₂.

Different appearance: An ester link may be hidden inside a ring or connect two large pieces.

A difference that matters: An ester can split into alcohol pieces; an amide follows a different bond pattern. The bulky reagent in 17.41(d) stops an acid chloride at an aldehyde.

Starter practice sequence: 17.35B(b) → 17.41(b) → 17.55

Repeat ester reduction, then open two ester links. Use 17.41(c) to contrast the amide rule.

P4 — Turn an alcohol into C=O—or an aldehyde into COOH

Notice first: Find OH or CHO and identify the oxidizing reagent.

The move: A secondary alcohol becomes a ketone. PCC changes a primary alcohol to an aldehyde; the aqueous strong oxidizing mixture can continue to COOH.

Different appearance: The same change may be a single product question or a middle step in synthesis.

A difference that matters: PCC, the silver reagent, and dichromate do not change the same set of groups. Count the carbon neighbors of the OH carbon.

Starter practice sequence: 17.37(e) → 17.37(f) → 17.42(c–e)

Repeat aldehyde oxidation, then compare which additional OH groups change.

P5 — Add one carbon group at C=O

Notice first: Find an aldehyde or ketone and the carbon carrying MgBr, Li, or a negative charge.

The move: Bond that incoming carbon to the C=O carbon. Turn the oxygen into OH after the finishing water step.

Different appearance: The incoming piece can be methyl, phenyl, an alkyne, or a larger chain. C=O can be in a ring.

A difference that matters: Use the carbon that carries the metal or charge. Do not add at a nearby C=C when the specified reagent instead adds at C=O.

Starter practice sequence: 17.37(g) → 17.37(h) → 17.37(j–k)

Repeat the same joining move with differently drawn carbon pieces. Then try the ring ketone in 17.43(b).

P6 — An ester or acid chloride can take two carbon groups

Notice first: Find an ester or acid chloride and excess Grignard or organolithium reagent.

The move: Remove the ester’s singly bonded OR piece, or the acid chloride’s Cl. Add two copies of the incoming carbon group at its C=O carbon, which ends with OH.

Different appearance: The ester may be attached to a ring or occur as one site in a larger structure.

A difference that matters: A ketone takes one group. A cuprate with an acid chloride stops at a ketone. The tether in 17.52 makes the second addition close a ring. The special conditions in 17.54 require separate treatment.

Starter practice sequence: 17.35B(c) → 17.43(d) → 17.70

Repeat the two-addition pattern, then recognize it from spectra. Contrast 17.35A(c).

P7 — Add at the far end of C=O–C=C

Notice first: Spot C=O–C=C and check for the cuprate reagent in these problems.

The move: Add the incoming group at the far alkene carbon. Make the original C=C single and keep C=O after the finishing step.

Different appearance: This connected pattern can be in a ring, a chain, or a molecule with another double bond.

A difference that matters: Grignard and organolithium reagents in the paired examples add at C=O instead. Keep any double bond in the incoming vinyl group.

Starter practice sequence: 17.38(c) → 17.43(e) → 17.40(f)

Repeat with ring and chain; then distinguish the original double bond from the incoming one.

Mapped EOC references: 17.38 (c); 17.40 (f); 17.43 (e); 17.67 (a); 17.67 (b); 17.78 Whole question.

P8 — Open the little oxygen ring

Notice first: Find the three-membered epoxide ring and compare its two carbons.

The move: For these basic carbon reagents, attach at the less crowded carbon and break that carbon’s bond to O. O stays on the other carbon and becomes OH. In 17.73, use the reverse connection pattern to close an epoxide from O⁻ and a neighboring C–Cl site.

Different appearance: The epoxide may be on a straight chain, a ring, or a fused-ring structure.

A difference that matters: The new group comes from the opposite side of the broken C–O bond. If the two ends are equivalent, check whether they still give different 3D products.

Starter practice sequence: 17.38(d) → 17.43(f) → 17.45(b)

Repeat ring opening, then make the opposite-face requirement explicit. Reverse the move in 17.61.

Mapped EOC references: 17.36 (d); 17.38 (d); 17.43 (f); 17.45 (b); 17.46 Whole question; 17.49 G; 17.61 (a); 17.61 (b); 17.61 (c); 17.63 (b); 17.73 Whole question.

P9 — Cover OH, do the carbon chemistry, uncover OH

Notice first: Look for an OH that must survive a strong carbon reagent.

The move: Replace the OH hydrogen with a protecting group. Do the needed reaction, then remove the cover to recover OH.

Different appearance: OH may be far from the bond you are trying to make. It still affects reagent compatibility.

A difference that matters: An exposed OH can use up the Grignard reagent. A permanent ether in the target is not a temporary protecting group.

Starter practice sequence: 17.37(l) → 17.48 → 17.66(a)

Learn the covering move, then use it to prevent a failed reaction and to build an alkyne.

Mapped EOC references: 17.37 (l); 17.48 Whole question; 17.66 (a); 17.66 (b); 17.68 Whole question; 17.74 Whole question.

P10 — Choose how many carbons to add

Notice first: Compare the starting skeleton with the target. Count the new carbons.

The move: A Grignard plus formaldehyde adds one carbon ending in OH. Ethylene oxide adds two ending in OH. CO₂ adds one ending in COOH.

Different appearance: The original piece may be straight, branched, aromatic, or cyclic.

A difference that matters: These three partners do not add the same number of carbons or give the same end group.

Starter practice sequence: 17.36(c) → 17.49F → 17.63(a)

Repeat the one-carbon OH extension. Contrast the two-carbon extension in 17.36(d) and COOH extension in 17.43(a).

P11 — Choose the face, not just the connection

Notice first: Check wedges, dashes, existing stereocenters, and any face-selective reagent.

The move: First make the correct connections. Then decide which side receives the new bond and which existing orientations stay.

Different appearance: The same product connection can require a mirror-image pair, several relative arrangements, or one favored face.

A difference that matters: CBS reagent labels do not give a universal opposite R/S product label. Existing groups and bridges can make the faces different.

Starter practice sequence: 17.39(a) → 17.39(b–c) → 17.47

Compare equal-face addition with a favored face; then use crowding to choose the approach.

P12 — Work backward from the target

Notice first: Find the target’s OH carbon, N-adjacent carbon, or new carbon–carbon bond.

The move: Undo a known forward move: cut the added carbon group away and restore the carbonyl or epoxide starting pattern. Then check the forward reaction.

Different appearance: The question may ask for starting materials, several routes, or a full synthesis from restricted ingredients.

A difference that matters: An ester route needs two matching added groups. A ring bond cannot always be cut into two separate starting molecules. Starting-material restrictions still apply.

Starter practice sequence: 17.57(a) → 17.57(b) → 17.59

Repeat backward carbonyl cuts; then impose the matching-pair requirement for an ester route.

S1 — Connect the main moves with earlier reactions

Notice first: Look for a necessary change such as OH to Br, Br to MgBr, alkene to epoxide, or alcohol to alkene.

The move: Use the earlier reaction that prepares the next step. Keep track of every intermediate. Also retain the specific rule from 17.38(a): a cuprate replaces acid-chloride Cl with one carbon group and stops at a ketone.

Different appearance: These moves are often buried inside a long synthesis rather than asked alone.

A difference that matters: Ring substitution position, carbon count, protecting groups, and reagent order can decide whether a route works.

Starter practice sequence: 17.49H–J → 17.63(a) → 17.64(b)

Repeat OH-to-Grignard preparation, then check why aromatic bromination must come later.

S2 — Use the product to guide electron arrows

Notice first: Mark the bond that must form or break, then find the electron pair that can make that change.

The move: Start each curved arrow at a bond or lone pair. Point it to the atom or bond receiving that pair, and check charges afterward.

Different appearance: An addition may be followed by ester opening, another addition, ring closing, or proton transfer.

A difference that matters: Knowing the product does not replace the intermediate steps. Do not draw an arrow starting at a positive charge.

Starter practice sequence: 17.51 → 17.53 → 17.55

Repeat addition and ester opening with different reagents. Then see a new ring close in 17.52.

S3 — Use spectra to check a proposed structure

Notice first: First use the reagent and formula to suggest a product. Then inspect the stated IR and NMR clues.

The move: IR helps identify groups such as C=O or OH. NMR signal sizes and splitting help check which H environments the structure contains.

Different appearance: Two compounds can have the same mass but different groups. Symmetry can make a large structure give few signals.

A difference that matters: Mass alone does not identify a structure. An OH peak is not a reliable substitute for counting H attached to the OH-bearing carbon.

Starter practice sequence: 17.49 F/G/K comparison → 17.69 → 17.70

Repeat the check of H near OH, then use symmetry and integration. 17.71–72 add less familiar reaction clues.

Mapped EOC references: 17.49 Tell F, G, and K apart by NMR; 17.69 A; 17.69 B; 17.70 E; 17.70 F; 17.71 Whole question; 17.72 Whole question; 17.75 Whole question.

S4 — Use another electron arrangement to find a reactive site

Notice first: Look for a charged carbon next to a double bond or a C=O–C=C system.

The move: Draw another allowed electron arrangement without moving the atoms. Use it to locate a reactive or electron-poor carbon.

Different appearance: This can explain a reaction at an unexpected carbon or a larger NMR shift farther from oxygen.

A difference that matters: Resonance drawings are not separate molecules that take turns existing. Check charges and carbon’s bond count.

Starter practice sequence: 17.75 → 17.77

Use the same electron-delocalization idea for a spectrum explanation and for an unexpected bond-forming site. These are different tasks, not identical practice.

Mapped EOC references: 17.72 Whole question; 17.75 Whole question; 17.77 N, the major product; 17.77 Mechanism idea.

S5 — Recognize a special pathway before using the usual shortcut

Notice first: Look for the unfamiliar starting group, unusual product, or special conditions in the prompt.

The move: Use the specific intermediate shown by the solution to connect the starting group to the product.

Different appearance: Examples include a nitrile becoming a ketone, an amide ending without oxygen, or one-addition ester conditions.

A difference that matters: Do not force every carbon reagent plus C=O into “finish with an alcohol.” Keep these exceptions attached to their question and conditions.

Starter practice sequence: 17.54; 17.71; 17.72; 17.77–79

These need separate mini-cards. They are not one interchangeable reaction family.

Mapped EOC references: 17.54 Whole question; 17.71 Whole question; 17.72 Whole question; 17.77 N, the major product; 17.77 Mechanism idea; 17.78 Whole question; 17.79 Whole question.

What this suggests we build first

1. One reusable decision guide: use P1 to choose which sites react, then link to the relevant move. This comes before hiding parts of a large molecule.

2. Three high-value contrasts: ketone versus ester; direct C=O addition versus far-end addition; unprotected OH versus protected OH. Each pair changes one important decision.

3. Repeated practice: take two or three existing examples of the same move before introducing the contrast. The sequences above are proposed selections from Smith, not newly invented questions.

4. A separate layer for face and mechanism: after the student knows the product connections, add the P11 face check or S2 arrow steps when the question requires them.

5. Small exception cards: keep 17.54 and 17.71–79 accessible without teaching their special pathways as universal rules.

Scope and review notes

Based on the uploaded Smith chapter 17 question and solutions PDFs and the preceding source-checked EOC companion draft. The solutions remain the main guide. Page numbers in the index distinguish the manual’s printed chapter-page labels from the question PDF’s file-page numbers. No new cross-textbook match has been asserted.

Coverage checks confirm 45 consecutive parent questions and one tag assignment for every separately identified entry in the companion. Multi-step tasks have overlapping tags. This does not prove that students can answer everything using these cards alone: earlier reaction knowledge, product drawing, complete mechanisms, and spectroscopy skills are still needed where indicated.

The grouping and practice order are teaching proposals for review. No student trial has yet established their effectiveness. The No Fear rewrite and changes to the app remain paused.
